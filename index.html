A small guide

A coda is the sound at the end of a syllable. For these nasal codas, a useful Middle Chinese sketch is:

-mKorean ㅁ · Japanese ん · Mandarin -n · Cantonese -m
-nKorean ㄴ · Japanese ん · Mandarin -n · Cantonese -n
-ŋKorean ㅇ · Japanese has more than one historical route · Mandarin -ng · Cantonese -ng

Then there is Yōkan.

羊羹 yōkan
vegetarian lamb cake
a lamb dish from which the lamb has been removed for several centuries

In one major Sino-Japanese route, old -ŋ gradually stopped behaving like a consonant, melting into long vowels. But later borrowing layers can preserve a different flavor. That is exactly why 羊羹 is such a nice specimen. [1]

羊羹 (Yōkan) is not a failed rule, but the cutest reminder that different borrowing layers can send the same old coda toward different endings. 羹 is read かん in this Tō-on layer, while 羊 → よう follows the long-vowel route.

Other sweet counterexamples

杏仁豆腐あんにんどうふ

杏 historically ended in -ŋ, but here it enters through the later あん reading. A dessert with a later borrowing layer baked into its name. [4]

杏子餡あんずあん

杏子 itself preserves a Tang-on-derived fruit name; add sugar and syrup, and the later layer literally becomes a sweet filling. [4]

Then ㅁ walks in.

感 looks perfectly ordinary in Mandarin: gǎn.

But Korean 감 and Cantonese gam² quietly point to an older -m.

Good 音感 helps.

Mandarin merged old -m with -n. Korean and Cantonese kept the nasal codas apart: /m n ŋ/. That makes words like 感 especially nice little fossils. [3]

Why linguists care

Each Sino-Xenic reading is a small slice of Chinese sound history, taken at a different moment of borrowing. Some preserve distinctions that later disappeared in Chinese. Historical phonologists put these slices side by side, trace the differences between them, and reconstruct earlier sound systems. [2]
