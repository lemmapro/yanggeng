A small coda guide

A coda is the sound at the end of a syllable. For these nasal codas, a useful Middle Chinese sketch is:

-mKorean ㅁ · Japanese ん · Mandarin -n · Cantonese -m
-nKorean ㄴ · Japanese ん · Mandarin -n · Cantonese -n
-ŋKorean ㅇ · Japanese has more than one historical route · Mandarin -ng · Cantonese -ng

Japanese: what melted?

In one major Sino-Japanese route, old -ŋ gradually stopped behaving like a consonant. Its nasal trace softened into the end of the vowel; then the nasality disappeared, leaving an extra i or u, which often fused into a long vowel. [1]

-ŋ
a consonant tail
→
nasal tail
the coda softens
→
i / u
nasality fades
→
long vowel
the tail melts in

But later borrowing layers can preserve a different route. That is exactly why 羊羹 is such a nice specimen.

羊羹 is not a failed rule. It is the cutest reminder that different borrowing layers can send the same old coda toward different endings.

羹 is read かん in this Tō-on layer, while 羊 → よう follows the long-vowel route.

one dessert, two routes, still sweet

Other sweet counterexamples

杏仁豆腐あんにんどうふ

杏 historically ended in -ŋ, but here it enters through the later あん reading. A dessert with a later borrowing layer baked into its name. [4]

杏子餡あんずあん

杏子 itself preserves a Tang-on-derived fruit name; add sugar and syrup, and the later layer literally becomes a sweet filling. [4]

Then ㅁ walks in.

感 looks perfectly ordinary in Mandarin: gǎn.

But Korean 감 and Cantonese gam² quietly point to an older -m.

Good 音感 helps.

Old -m merged with -n in Mandarin, but Korean still keeps ㅁ and Cantonese still keeps an /m n ŋ/ coda contrast. That makes words like 感 especially nice little fossils. [3]

Why linguists care

Sino-Xenic readings preserve snapshots from different moments of Chinese sound history. That is why historical phonologists can use Korean, Japanese, and Vietnamese readings as evidence when reconstructing earlier Chinese pronunciation. [2]
